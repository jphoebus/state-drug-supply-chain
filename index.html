Who sets the price of a prescription?

How Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, Maryland, and Georgia regulate the businesses between a drug's manufacturer and the patient: pharmacy benefit managers, drug prices, and 340B contract pharmacy arrangements. Current as of October 16, 2026.

Pharmacy benefit managers: all five states regulate PBMs through their insurance regulators. The newer laws move from banning specific practices toward setting how PBMs price drugs, usually starting with public plans such as Medicaid and state employee coverage.

Drug prices and 340B: Maryland is the only one of the five with an affordability board or a contract pharmacy law. Legislatures in the other states have debated both. Virginia's legislature passed affordability board bills three years in a row, and governors of both parties vetoed them. On 340B, Ohio enacted reporting by program participants and Virginia ordered a study.

Three terms, briefly

Pharmacy benefit managers

Companies that run prescription drug benefits for insurers, employers, and public programs. They negotiate rebates with manufacturers, set what pharmacies are paid, and manage formularies, the lists of drugs a plan covers. States regulate them mainly through insurance law.

Affordability boards

State boards that review the cost of specific high-priced drugs. Some only study and recommend. Others can set an upper payment limit, the most that covered purchasers in the state will pay for a drug.

340B contract pharmacies

Under a federal program, drug manufacturers that take part in Medicaid must offer discounted prices to certain hospitals and clinics, which often dispense through outside pharmacies. Since 2020, many manufacturers have limited discounts on drugs sent to those pharmacies; some states have passed laws barring those limits.

What stands out

PBM rules are converging, at different speeds

Every state licenses or registers PBMs, or will soon (Ohio's new license is required from July 2027), and states limit practices such as steering patients to PBM-owned pharmacies. Some states now regulate the pricing model itself: Virginia will require pass-through pricing and full rebate pass-through from 2027, while Pennsylvania's 2024 law orders a study of spread pricing.

Public plans go first

Ohio and Virginia use or are moving to a single Medicaid PBM, and Georgia set its pricing rules first for state and local government plans. States can set terms as purchasers before they set them as regulators.

Drug price authority is the dividing line

Maryland's board can cap what state and local government plans pay for a drug, with a path to wider use. Board bills were vetoed in Virginia, did not pass in Georgia, are pending in Ohio, and were not introduced in Pennsylvania this session.

340B is moving to the courts and to study

Maryland's contract pharmacy law is in effect while the full Fourth Circuit reconsiders a manufacturer challenge. Virginia created a work group, and Ohio required hospitals and clinics in the program to report how they use it.

Reporting is the most common tool

Where states have not set prices, they have asked for information: PBM transparency reports in Pennsylvania, manufacturer price reports in Virginia, payment reports in Georgia, and 340B reports in Ohio.

What to watch

  • Pending: the full Fourth Circuit's rehearing of the challenge to Maryland's 340B contract pharmacy law (argument date not confirmed).
  • November 1, 2026: Virginia's 340B work group report.
  • December 31, 2026: Maryland's PBM workgroup report; Ohio's General Assembly session ends with HB 890 and the 340B bills in committee.
  • January 1, 2027: State and local government purchasers may begin applying Maryland's first upper payment limits, for Jardiance and Ozempic, if the regulations are final; they must apply them after January 1, 2028.
  • 2027 sessions: whether Virginia's legislature takes up an affordability board a fourth time, and whether Pennsylvania's HB 2797 on 340B moves.
  • July 1, 2027: Virginia's pass-through pricing requirement and Ohio's PBM license requirement take effect.

The question I expect to shape the next cycle: whether states keep regulating the drug supply chain one link at a time, or start treating pricing across PBMs, manufacturers, and pharmacies as a single decision.

How this was compiled

Scope. Five states, three areas: pharmacy benefit manager regulation, drug price authority and reporting, and 340B contract pharmacy laws. Each cell describes enacted law first and notes notable 2025–26 bills separately.

Sources. Facts are drawn from statutes, session laws, legislative records, regulator and board pages, and court orders. Several legislature sites block automated access; where a bill's history comes from a site that copies official records, the source list links that copy. Where a detail rests on a secondary source, the table says "as reported." Where a detail could not be confirmed, the page says so rather than guessing.

Neutrality. Drug manufacturers, PBMs, insurers, pharmacies, hospitals, and patient groups all have stakes in these laws. This page describes what states have decided and how, not whether those decisions are right.
